# One Ear, Two Kids, and Fifty Pints of Gratitude

*A personal reflection on microtia, becoming a father, and finding purpose in giving back*

---

I was born with microtia.

That's the medical term for it. In practice, it meant I came into the world without a left ear — not just missing the canal, but the whole outer structure. Just smooth skin where an ear should have been. For most of my childhood, I didn't call it anything. I just knew I was different, and that being different in a visible way when you're young is its own particular kind of hard.

I wore my hair longer than I wanted to. I'd position myself a certain way in photos — always favouring my right side. In a room full of people, I was quietly calculating angles. It sounds exhausting looking back, and I suppose it was, though at the time it just felt normal. It was just what I did.

I wasn't unhappy. I want to be honest about that. I had a good childhood, a family who loved me, friends, the usual mess of growing up. But underneath all of it there was this low hum of self-consciousness that I couldn't quite shake. A feeling, hard to put into words, that I was a little bit incomplete.

In a literal sense, I was.

---

## The Operation

The surgery to reconstruct my ear wasn't an emergency. It wasn't going to save my life. Medically speaking, microtia is classified as a condition that affects appearance and sometimes hearing — but it's not dangerous. Which meant that for years I told myself I didn't really *need* the operation. That I should just get on with things. That wanting it fixed was somehow vain or weak.

I've since come to think that was just fear with a more respectable name.

When I finally had the surgery — and it's a significant procedure, usually involving cartilage reconstruction over multiple stages — I wasn't prepared for how I'd feel afterwards. I'd expected relief, maybe. What I actually felt was something closer to peace. Standing in front of a mirror, I didn't think *now I look normal*. I thought: *I did something for myself*. Something I'd put off for far too long.

It's a strange thing, finally letting yourself be helped. The surgeon didn't just reconstruct an ear. In some way I'm still trying to articulate, he helped me stop treating myself as an afterthought.

Rilke wrote something that's stayed with me since: *"Perhaps all the dragons in our lives are princesses who are only waiting to see us act, just once, with beauty and courage."* I'd spent a long time treating my ear — and my feelings about it — as a dragon to be avoided. Turns out it was just something I needed to stop looking away from.

---

## Becoming a Father

My first child arrived not long after the operation.

I don't think any amount of preparation really gets you ready for that moment. Holding them for the first time, I felt everything rearrange itself — priorities, fears, the stories I'd been telling about myself. I thought about the boy I'd been. The one who angled himself in photographs and grew his hair too long. And I felt this very clear, almost startling sense that I didn't want to pass that kind of quiet self-diminishment on to my child.

That I wanted to be someone they could look at and see a person who faced things.

The timing felt significant — though I'll admit that might just be the way we make meaning out of coincidence. Still, the surgery and the birth landed so close together that they've always felt linked in my mind. Two things that arrived almost simultaneously and pointed in the same direction: *pay attention, this matters, step up*.

Baldwin puts it better than I could: *"Not everything that is faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced."* Fatherhood, more than anything else in my life, made avoidance feel like something I simply couldn't afford.

---

## Then There Were Four

My second child came a few years later.

With my first, if I'm honest, I was still figuring out the basics. Everything felt high-stakes. Every small decision carried an enormous weight I probably didn't need to give it. I was trying hard, which is good, but I was also tense in the way that first-time parents often are — like you might break something if you're not paying close enough attention.

My second child brought perspective. By the time my second child arrived, I'd relaxed enough to understand that parenting isn't really about getting it right every time. It's about showing up, consistently, over years. It's about the small things more than the big gestures.

I'm 42 now. I know that's not old, but it's old enough to have a sense of how quickly time actually moves — and what you want to be doing with it. What I keep coming back to is this: my kids will grow up watching how I live, not just hearing what I say. That thought doesn't fill me with dread the way it might have done ten years ago. If anything, it feels like a useful anchor.

---

## Fifty Units

A few weeks after the surgery, I signed up to give blood.

It was a simple impulse, really. I felt grateful — to the NHS, to the medical team, to the fact that I live in a country where something like this is possible — and I wanted to do something with that gratitude that was more than just a feeling. Giving blood felt like the most direct translation I could find: someone helped me; now I can help someone else.

I had no particular plan beyond that first donation.

That was fifty units ago.

Fifty times I've made the appointment, turned up, sat in the chair, and rolled up my sleeve. Each session takes about an hour. A single donation can [help save up to three lives](https://www.blood.co.uk/why-give-blood/how-your-blood-is-used/). I find that genuinely staggering, if I stop to think about it — the idea that something my body produces naturally, and replenishes on its own, could make that kind of difference to someone in a hospital bed somewhere.

I don't say that to make it sound more heroic than it is. It isn't heroic. It's an hour of sitting still a few times a year. Most people could do it. I'm simply one of the people who does.

What I do want to be clear about is that this isn't a chapter I'm closing. Reaching fifty felt like a moment worth reflecting on — hence this piece — but the plan is to carry on for as long as I'm eligible and my health allows. The need doesn't stop, so the commitment doesn't either. Somewhere, on some ordinary Tuesday, someone is going to need what I can give. I'd like to still be giving it.

---

## What I Believe In

I'm not religious. I don't have a faith that tells me why I'm here or what I'm supposed to be doing with my life. I've made peace with that — but it does mean I've had to find my own version of purpose, built out of the things I actually believe in rather than the things I was told to.

What I believe in is pretty simple. I believe in being present for my kids. I believe in doing things that help people, even in small and unglamorous ways. I believe in the idea that you can spend a lifetime being afraid of the things that make you different, or you can eventually decide that those things are just part of the story — not the whole of it.

I was born with microtia. I had an operation that changed how I felt about myself. I became a father twice over, and both times it shifted something fundamental. I've given fifty units of blood because, around the same time, a surgeon helped change how I saw myself and my first child arrived into the world. Together, those things made me want to be useful in whatever way I could. around the same time and made me want to be useful in whatever way I could.

None of that is dramatic. It's just a life, made up of choices and moments and the slow accumulation of small things that, from a distance, start to look like something.

I was born missing something. I spent a long time thinking that was the most important fact about me.

It wasn't.

---

*If this resonated with you and you're based in the UK, I'd gently encourage you to look into giving blood. You can find out if you're eligible and book an appointment at [give.blood.nhs.uk](https://www.blood.co.uk). It's about an hour of your time. It might be the most useful thing you do this month.*
