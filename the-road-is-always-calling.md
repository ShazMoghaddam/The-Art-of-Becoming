# The Road Is Always Calling

*On riding, growing up, and the things we pass on without even realising it*

---

I was maybe six years old. My dad lifted me up in front of him on the seat, put his arms either side of me, and the engine started. I felt it before I heard it — a low, steady rumble, like something waking beneath us.

Then we moved.

The wind hit my face and I gasped. Not from fear — from how alive it felt. We were on a quiet country road, hedgerows either side, the kind of twisty lane that bends away and leaves you wondering what's around the next corner. For a few seconds I felt weightless. Like the road had no end and we were just going to keep following it, wherever it led.

I've been chasing that feeling ever since.

---

## I. It Gets Into You Early

My dad wasn't a casual rider. Bikes were just part of who he was. Each one he owned over the years told you something about where he was in life — what he needed, what he was working through, who he was trying to be. I grew up watching that and picked something up without realising: that some people just need to move. And the best thing they can do is be honest about it.

There was always a quiet rebellion in riding, and I won't pretend otherwise. Not the dangerous kind. Just the feeling of stepping outside the ordinary for a while. Of saying, without words, *this is mine*. It's why shows like *Sons of Anarchy* connected with so many riders — not for the drama, but for the brotherhood and the sense that the road was the one place you could just be. Charlie Hunnam carried that same searching quality into *Shantaram* — a man on a bike in a foreign country, finding himself through motion. I recognised something in that.

When I finally got my licence, I'd been ready for years. The first time I pulled away on my own — no dad, no safety net, just me and a country road stretching ahead — I didn't feel brave. I felt settled. Like something had quietly clicked.

Riding doesn't let you drift. You can't run through your worries at sixty miles an hour. The road asks for all of you, and in return, it clears everything out. Out there, I wasn't anyone's son, colleague, or obligation. I was just a rider. On the best mornings — light low, road empty, *Go Your Own Way* by Fleetwood Mac coming through my helmet speakers — that was more than enough.

A GPS trace shows you where you went. It can't show you what you came back as.

---

## II. The Same Freedom, Different Shapes

I truly believe the thing that gets a person on a motorbike is the same thing that gets someone up before sunrise to paddle out before the sea gets choppy. Or sends a teenager down a hill on a longboard. Or has a cyclist clipping in at dawn, chasing a climb they've failed before.

The vehicle almost doesn't matter. What matters is the movement, and what it asks of you.

A cyclist shifting their weight through a gradient is doing the same thing a motorcyclist does leaning into a twisty bend — both thinking through their hands and feet, not their heads. Both have let go of something. *Life Is a Highway* by Tom Cochrane works on a bike, a board, a wave, because the feeling doesn't belong to any one thing. It belongs to the state of going.

Paddleboarding taught me this more clearly than I expected. The ocean doesn't stay still. It pushes back, shifts, has its own moods. You learn to read it the same way you read a road — with your whole body, quietly adjusting. Fight it and you fall. Work with it and something opens up. That's where the good stuff is.

Longboarding is the same. A clean carve down a hill isn't about strength — it's about letting go at exactly the right moment. You lean, and gravity does the rest. The skill is trusting that lean.

Any movement that teaches you to trust yourself a little more is worth making time for.

---

## III. What You Pass Down Without Planning To

Life filled up. Work, marriage, kids. The bike stayed. Some things just do.

What I didn't see coming was the morning I looked in my mirrors and saw my kids back there — cheeks flushed, eyes wide, laughing into the wind. I had become my dad. Not in a scary way. In the best possible way.

We ride together now on quiet weekend mornings — past villages, down back lanes, through places that look completely different when you're not sealed behind glass. Down roads that open suddenly onto a view you weren't expecting. I watch their faces and I see it: that exact moment when their shoulders drop, their eyes go soft, and the world gets a little bigger.

*Radar Love* by Golden Earring fits those mornings perfectly — that long, rolling feeling of covering ground, of moving through something and coming out changed. That's what I hope they carry. Not the bike. Just that feeling.

What I'm giving them isn't a hobby. It's permission. To find the things that make them feel like themselves — really themselves, not the version they put on for everyone else. To hold onto those things, even when life suggests otherwise.

---

Then there are the small moments that happen when you stop.

I've pulled up outside a café on a coastal road, helmet off, the sea somewhere just below, and had a complete stranger walk over — not a biker, just someone with a morning coffee — and say, *"She's beautiful,"* nodding toward the bike. And then we talked for twenty minutes. About nothing particular. About everything. There's something about a motorbike parked near the sea that makes people pause. Not because it's showy. Because it stirs something. A memory. A version of themselves they'd shelved. A conversation they didn't know they needed.

Those small connections are some of my favourite parts of riding. They never make the highlight reel. But they're real, and they stay with you long after the ride.

---

## IV. The World Rides Too

I've been lucky enough to ride in different countries, and what I carry from those trips isn't the scenery — though some of it has been breathtaking, coastal roads with the sea far below and nothing between you and the horizon. What I carry is the culture. How differently biking expresses itself from place to place, and yet how quickly you recognise the same thing underneath it all.

In some places it's the craft — handbuilt machines with details that took months. In others, it's community: riders arriving at the same spot every Sunday morning simply because that's what you do. Everywhere I've gone, the welcome has been the same. You ride up, someone nods, the conversation starts. No introduction needed.

Back home, I've wandered through the **Bike Shed Moto Show** in London — custom bikes that are practically sculpture, people who care about doing things their own way. The Bike Shed now has a home in Los Angeles too, and watching that same spirit cross an ocean and land just as naturally reminded me this isn't a hobby. It's a language.

I've spent evenings at **Ace Café London**, where bikes and music and decades of stories all sit together in one building. People have been meeting there since 1938. It doesn't feel like nostalgia. It feels like continuity — proof that riders have always found each other, and always will.

**Goodwood Road & Racing** stirs something different. Machines from every era gathered in one place, the smell of old fuel and fresh grass, the sound of engines that haven't run in years suddenly alive again. You feel the whole long line of it — the love passed down through generations of people who simply couldn't help themselves.

And then there's the **Isle of Man TT**. I won't try to explain it fully — words don't quite reach. Riders at impossible speed on open roads, stone walls inches away. What gets me isn't the bravery. It's the commitment. Watching it live for the first time, I understood something about passion I'd never quite been able to name. MotoGP carries that same thread, scaled to a stadium — different energy, same love underneath.

Wherever you find it — a polished showroom in East London, a sun-baked circuit in Valencia, a car park on the Isle of Man at six in the morning — the feeling is always the same. You are among your people.

Every bar is someone who needed to move — and found out they weren't the only one.

---

## V. We Already Speak the Same Language

Every spring, in cities across the world, riders appear — not in racing gear, but in tweed and polished boots, on gleaming bikes — and ride together. The **Distinguished Gentleman's Ride** started on one street in Sydney in 2012. Now it crosses dozens of countries and has raised millions for men's mental health and prostate cancer research.

What moves me about it isn't the spectacle. It's the proof. That riding is not the closed-off, solitary thing it sometimes gets painted as. You get on a bike in London and you already share something with a rider in Tokyo, Cape Town, or Buenos Aires. Something unspoken. Something neither of you has to explain.

*Bitter Sweet Symphony* by The Verve has always felt like a riding song to me, even though it isn't one. It's about moving through life — not always on your terms, not always gracefully, but moving. That's the spirit: people showing up, choosing to be present, finding each other briefly on a country road or a seafront car park or a street they've never been on before.

I've felt it myself, ride after ride — you leave tense and come back clear. Not because anything changed out there. Because you remembered something about yourself that was always true.

---

## VI. Still Moving

On a recent evening ride — one of those where the light turns golden and a quiet valley road looks like it was put there just for this — I thought about my dad. About being six, holding on in front of him, not scared at all, already in love with something I didn't have a name for.

He didn't explain it. He just took me with him. And somehow that was enough.

It took me a long time to understand what he actually gave me. It wasn't just the bikes. It was a way back to myself — when life gets heavy, when the noise builds, when I lose the thread. Getting on the bike doesn't fix anything. It just puts me back in the present, fully here, for the length of that ride. And that's all I need.

My kids will find their own versions of this. Their own roads, rhythms, ways of coming back when they've drifted. I can't choose those for them. But I hope — I really hope — they carry this: that there's always a way through, and it usually involves moving. That the soul doesn't do well standing still for too long.

The engine turns over.

The road opens up.

Some things you pass down without meaning to. The best things, you pass down on purpose.

## 🎬 Worth Watching

- **Long Way Round / Long Way Down / Long Way Up** — Ewan McGregor and Charley Boorman, two friends, two bikes, and thousands of miles of honest road. The best argument for just going.
- **Sons of Anarchy** — Not a documentary, but the brotherhood feels real. Charlie Hunnam understood the culture.
- **Shantaram** — Charlie Hunnam on a bike in Bombay, searching for something. Atmospheric, restless, and quietly beautiful.
- **MotoBob on YouTube** — No frills, just good content and honest reviews.
