# The Weight of Enough

There's a question that's been sitting quietly on the back of my mind for a while now. It isn't based on research or data. It's just something I keep noticing in the world around me, and in myself.

I'll start with the honest part first: I'm not writing this from outside the thing I'm questioning. I'm in it. I've bought the shoes I didn't need because the ad caught me at the right moment. I've felt that little lift when a package arrives, and I've felt it fade by the following week, sometimes by the following day. So this isn't a piece about other people's habits. It's about a pattern I keep noticing in mine, and then noticing everywhere else once I've seen it in myself.

## I. The Language We Use to Feel Better About It

Our wardrobes are already full. Our garages, our shelves, our inboxes. I opened mine one morning and realised I wasn't choosing between absence and abundance. I was choosing between versions of enough — different colours of a decision I'd already made a dozen times over.

Here's the thing that first got under my skin, though. It wasn't the obvious over-consumption — the fast fashion hauls, the constant upgrading. It was subtler than that. It was watching clothing brands build entire campaigns around sustainability while still releasing new collections every few weeks. The language says *conscious*, *considered*, *responsible*. The actual behaviour underneath it hasn't changed much at all. More is still being made. More is still expected to sell.

And then there's the second-hand economy, which I actually love in principle — the idea of giving something a longer life instead of a landfill. But watching it turn into its own fast-moving marketplace, with its own hype cycles and resale flipping and "just dropped" culture, made me wonder if we've found a way to keep the entire machine running, just with a better conscience attached. Buying secondhand can be a genuinely good instinct. But when the platform itself starts behaving like the thing it was supposed to be an alternative to — new drops, algorithmic feeds, the same dopamine loop — I start to wonder whether we've solved anything or just relabelled it.

### When Sustainability Becomes a Marketing Language

That's really what unsettles me most. Not that companies lie exactly — most of the claims are technically true in some narrow sense. It's that "sustainable" has quietly become a tone of voice more than a description of behaviour. I noticed it properly the first time I caught myself reading a label, feeling reassured by the word, and not actually checking anything underneath it. That's the whole trick, maybe. The idea gets absorbed. The system doesn't change shape; it just changes vocabulary.

I don't say this from a place of certainty, though. I'm aware that some of this really is progress — better materials, real reductions in waste, actual improvements sitting underneath the marketing. I just find it hard to tell, from the outside, which parts are the substance and which parts are the sentence structure.

## II. Pace as the Product

The other thing I keep circling back to is technology, and specifically how fast it moves now. Drones are a strange example, but a useful one — every few months there's a new model, marginally better camera, marginally longer flight time, marginally smarter obstacle avoidance. And I find myself asking, genuinely, whether the improvement is the point anymore, or whether the *pace* itself has become the product. Whether companies aren't really selling you a better drone so much as selling you the feeling of being current, which expires by design a little faster each cycle.

I caught myself doing this recently — sitting there watching reviews for a newer version of something that was still working perfectly beside me. Nothing was broken. Nothing had failed me. I just wanted to know what I was supposedly missing.

I don't think this is unique to drones. Phones, laptops, kitchen gadgets — the interval between "new" and "obsolete" keeps shrinking, and I don't think that shrinking is accidental. It's not that the old thing broke. It's that the new thing arrived, and arriving is enough to make the old one feel finished.

Which brings me to something I keep turning over: we seem, increasingly, to invent solutions to problems that didn't quite exist before the solution did. A gadget shows up, and only afterward does the "need" for it get explained to us, clearly and convincingly, until it's hard to remember not having it. I'm not accusing anyone of bad faith here — I think most of this happens without a villain in the room. It's just what a system built on constant output eventually does. It runs out of obvious problems and becomes increasingly skilled at turning inconveniences into markets.

## III. Objects That Used to Build Character

There's an older idea I keep coming back to, one I can't fully defend with evidence but can't quite let go of either: that objects used to build character over time, the way people do. A leather bag that softened and darkened with use. A tool that took the shape of your hand after enough years. A coat that got mended instead of replaced, and the mending became part of its story rather than a flaw in it.

I'm aware this might just be nostalgia wearing the costume of an argument — every era throws away its flimsy things, and only the well-made survivors reach us, so of course the past looks more solid from here. That's a fair challenge to my own instinct, and I don't have a clean answer to it. But even granting that, there's something real in the *idea* of an object earning its place through years of use, rather than being replaced the moment something marginally newer appears. Repair culture, where it still exists, seems to carry some of that. Cobblers, tailors, the guy two streets from me who still fixes watches, patiently, for people half his age who could've just bought a new one. It's a smaller economy than it used to be, but it hasn't disappeared, and I find something quietly reassuring in the fact that it hasn't.

## IV. Wanting More Isn't Always the Wrong Instinct

I want to be fair to the other side of this, because I think my instinct here is easy to romanticise and harder to justify. Someone who grew up without enough — without reliable clothing, without security, without the comfort of options — isn't wrong to want more, and it would be a strange kind of privilege for someone who's never known real scarcity to lecture them about the virtue of wanting less. "Enough" is a much easier philosophy to hold once you already have it. I recognise myself in that sentence more than I'd like to.

And progress genuinely has given us things worth keeping. Medicine, communication, the ability to reach someone on the other side of the world in seconds. None of what I'm circling here is really an argument against progress. It's something narrower — a question about whether we've confused the *pace* of change with the *value* of it. Whether we've started treating "new" as a virtue in itself, separate from whether the new thing is actually better, or just different enough to notice.

## V. Where Permanence Went

Maybe permanence hasn't disappeared so much as moved. Maybe it's less in objects now and more in other places — ideas that persist online, communities that stretch across distance in ways that weren't possible before, relationships that don't need proximity to last. I think that's probably true, at least partly.

But I don't think it fully answers the discomfort I started with. Because even accounting for all of that, there's still something in the *rhythm* of daily life that feels less settled than it could be. Fewer long pauses. Fewer things kept simply because they still work, rather than because they're still new.

> *"Enough is a feast." — Buddhist proverb*

I came across that line years ago, and it's stayed with me longer than most quotes do. It's such a small sentence, and such a difficult one to actually live inside. I can agree with it completely and still feel that flicker of wanting something I already have a version of, twenty minutes later, for no reason I can fully explain.

I don't have a tidy conclusion here, and I'd be suspicious of myself if I did. What I have instead are questions I'm still sitting with, genuinely unresolved, not as a rhetorical device but because I haven't worked them out.

When did enough stop being enough — or did it ever really start? Is the discomfort I feel about all this a real cultural shift, or just the ordinary restlessness of being human, dressed in newer language and faster delivery? When did we start replacing instead of repairing, instead of cherishing — and am I even entitled to use a word like *cherishing*, given how easily I still reach for the next thing myself? And if progress and acquisition have become almost inseparable in most of our minds, is that a flaw in how we live, or simply the price of a world most of us wouldn't actually trade back?

I'm starting to suspect "enough" isn't a place you arrive at and stay. It's something you keep asking, quietly, again and again — not because the answer changes, but because you do.
