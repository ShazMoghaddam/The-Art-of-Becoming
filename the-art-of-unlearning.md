# THE ART OF UNLEARNING

## A personal essay on letting go of what no longer fits

### Gathered by Shaz Moghaddam

---

There comes a point when you realise that growth isn't only about what you add.

Sometimes, the real work begins when you start letting go.

I didn't understand this for a long time. I kept building — adding knowledge, habits, goals, opinions. I had notebooks full of ideas, shelves full of books, a head full of plans. I thought that was what becoming someone meant. That the more you accumulated, the more solid you would feel.

But somewhere along the way, some of what I was carrying had started to weigh on me. Not all of it. Just certain things I'd held for so long I'd stopped noticing they were there.

That's when I began to understand something I hadn't expected: not everything we hold onto still belongs to us.

Unlearning is not forgetting who you are. It is quietly removing what no longer fits the person you are becoming.

---

### I. THE THINGS WE HOLD ONTO

We spend years collecting beliefs, habits, fears, and ways of thinking — some from family, some from the world around us, and some from experiences that quietly shaped us before we even knew what was happening. Over time, these things start to feel like identity. We stop questioning them because they've been around so long they feel like home.

But familiar isn't always the same as right.

> "The more a thing tends to be permanent, the more it tends to be lifeless."
> — Alan Watts

I came across that quote years ago and didn't fully understand it. Over time, it started to make sense. We hold onto old versions of ourselves because they feel safe. We repeat routines, thoughts, and behaviours simply because they are familiar — even when they no longer help us.

Sometimes we outgrow beliefs we once defended strongly. Sometimes we realise that fear has been making quiet decisions for us in the background. Sometimes we notice we've been living according to expectations that were never truly ours to begin with.

That realisation can be unsettling.

But it can also be a relief.

---

### II. THE DISCOMFORT OF QUESTIONING YOURSELF

Unlearning often begins with small moments. A conversation that shifts something. A period of silence. A mistake. A disappointment. A season of change that doesn't go the way you planned.

I remember a period in my own life when things that had always felt certain started to feel less so. Not because anything catastrophic had happened. But because I had started asking questions I'd been quietly avoiding.

*Why do I react this way?*
*Why does this fear keep showing up?*
*Why do I need so much approval?*
*Why am I afraid to slow down?*

I remember writing some of those questions down late one evening, just to look at them properly. And then sitting there not knowing what to do with them. There was no sudden clarity. No moment where everything made sense. Just the uncomfortable feeling of having named something I'd been carrying without realising it, and not yet knowing what to do next. That in-between space — where you've seen something you can't unsee, but haven't worked out what it means yet — is one of the harder places to sit. But I think it's also where most real change quietly begins.

One of the hardest things to unlearn is the belief that our worth depends on constant achievement, and it doesn't announce itself loudly — it just shows up in how uneasy you feel when you're not being productive, or how quickly you compare yourself to someone else without even meaning to. Many of us grow up quietly convinced we have to keep proving ourselves, and by the time we notice it, the habit is already deep.

And yet, some of the most settled people I've known are not the ones who achieved the most. They are the ones who stopped running from themselves.

> "Not everything that is faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced."
> — James Baldwin

There is a lot of honesty in that. Before anything can shift, we first have to be willing to see it clearly.

---

### III. HUMILITY AND HEALING

Unlearning also requires a kind of humility I wasn't always comfortable with. It means accepting that we may have been wrong about certain things. That some opinions we held confidently were built on a limited understanding of ourselves and the world.

That is not weakness. That is how people actually grow.

In many ways, unlearning is connected to healing. We carry emotional habits from difficult experiences without always noticing. Some people learn to hide how they feel because vulnerability once felt unsafe. Others learn to please everyone around them because rejection once hurt more than they knew how to carry.

Over time, survival patterns become personality traits. And after a while, we forget which is which.

That's worth sitting with. Because some of what we call our personality is actually just protection. Armour that made sense once. Habits built around wounds we never fully addressed. Not character flaws — just old responses to old pain, still running quietly in the background.

Recognising that doesn't mean tearing everything down. It just means looking more honestly at what we're carrying and asking whether we still need it.

> "We must be willing to let go of the life we have planned, so as to have the life that is waiting for us."
> — Joseph Campbell

I think many of us spend years holding tightly to a version of ourselves we decided on too early. Unlearning slowly loosens that grip. Not all at once. But piece by piece, season by season.

---

### IV. BECOMING MORE HONEST

People change, and the things they thought mattered most quietly rearrange themselves over time — what once felt urgent starts to feel hollow, and what once seemed out of reach starts to feel like exactly where you need to go. That's not failure. That's just life doing what life does.

There is a pressure today to always appear certain. To have a clean answer. To perform a version of yourself that holds together in every room. But real life is far more complicated than that. Most people are quietly figuring things out behind the scenes.

Sometimes wisdom isn't found in certainty.
Sometimes it's found in being willing to not know.

The older I get, the more I've come to believe that peace often comes from simplification. Less pretending. Less comparison. Less noise. Less pressure to have everything settled before you've even had the chance to live it.

Unlearning helps us return to something quieter and more honest in ourselves.

Not the version shaped by fear or expectation.
The version that was there before all of that.

I don't think this work ever fully finishes. There is probably always something else to question, some layer still left to look at. But that's not a discouraging thought. It just means the process of becoming is ongoing. That there is always more room to be more honest with yourself.

And maybe that's the real work of growth.

Not becoming someone completely different.

But becoming more truthful about who we already are.
