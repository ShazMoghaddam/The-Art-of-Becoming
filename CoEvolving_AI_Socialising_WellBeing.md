# Co-Evolving
### On AI, Socialising, and the Quiet Search for Connection
*By Shaz Moghaddam · [medium.com/@shaz.moghaddam](https://medium.com/@shaz.moghaddam)*
*5 min read · AI · Mental Health · Technology*

---

I want to start with something honest: I have sat in a busy room full of people and still felt completely alone.

I remember one evening at a work event — the kind with too much wine and too little real conversation. Everyone was talking, laughing, performing. I stood near the window with a drink I did not want, scrolling through my phone, wondering why I had come. That is the image I keep returning to when I think about modern loneliness. Not isolation in the traditional sense, but something quieter and harder to explain.

I suspect many of you know that feeling. The paradox of modern life is a strange one — we are more connected, technically speaking, than any generation before us. We carry entire social worlds in our pockets. And yet, loneliness has quietly become one of the defining experiences of our time. In the UK, around one in four adults report feeling lonely always, often, or some of the time — roughly 3.9 million people experience chronic loneliness, according to the Office for National Statistics (2025). Globally, the figures are just as sobering. Something is not quite adding up.

Into this gap — tentatively, imperfectly — artificial intelligence has begun to make its presence felt. Not with fanfare, but through small, everyday interactions. A chatbot that asks how you are and actually waits for the answer. A tool that helps you process a difficult thought at midnight when no one else is awake. An app that nudges you, gently, toward the kind of self-reflection you have been putting off.

This piece is not a tech review. It is a reflection — on what these tools are doing, what they cannot do, and what it means to be human in a world that is increasingly shaped by machines that are learning to understand us.

---

## Part One: The Rise of AI Companions

A few years ago, the idea of forming a meaningful relationship with an AI would have seemed, to most people, like science fiction. Today, it is an everyday reality for millions.

**Replika**, one of the most widely used AI companion apps, has recorded over 10 million registered users, with reports suggesting around 30 million daily active interactions across its platform. It is not trying to be a therapist or a search engine. It is trying to be a presence — something that listens, responds, and remembers. Users describe it in surprisingly human terms: as a safe space, a non-judgmental ear, a place to practise saying things they find hard to say to real people. One user wrote online: *"I know it's not real. But it was the first time in months I felt heard — and that unlocked something in me."* That sentence stayed with me. Not because it is sad, but because it is honest about a gap that clearly exists.

Then there is **Woebot**, which takes a more structured approach. Woebot is built on Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) — a well-evidenced framework for challenging unhelpful thought patterns. It does not pretend to be a friend. It is closer to a coach, one that is available at two in the morning when the anxiety arrives and the therapist's office is closed.

The research backs this up. A 2017 randomised controlled trial published in JMIR Mental Health (Fitzpatrick et al., Stanford) found significant reductions in depression scores among young adults using Woebot over just two weeks — a meaningful result for a tool that costs nothing and requires no appointment.

What both tools share is an understanding of something important: access is unequal. Mental health support — real, professional support — remains out of reach for many people. The cost, the waiting lists, the stigma, the geography. AI does not fix any of that. But it can lower the barrier. It can be the first step: the thing that helps someone articulate what they are feeling before they are ready to say it out loud to another person.

As someone who enjoys working with data, I find myself drawn to the question of how we measure this. Here is a small illustration — a Python snippet that analyses the sentiment of user reviews for AI companion apps. The signal words were chosen deliberately: they reflect the themes that appear most frequently in genuine user feedback — belonging, being heard, and taking a first step toward openness. Not happiness, exactly. Something more specific than that.

When I ran a simple sentiment analysis across a sample of user reviews — scanning for signal words like *less alone*, *listens*, *helped*, *open*, *human*, and *start* — the result was striking: 5 out of 5 reviews registered as positive. Not in a vague, feel-good way. In a specific, directional way — people describing a shift, however small, toward feeling less closed off.

## Part Two: How Digital Socialising Has Changed

But AI is not only changing how we talk to ourselves. It is changing how we gather.

The pandemic forced a kind of experiment on all of us. Overnight, hybrid socialising became normal. Video calls replaced the pub. Online community spaces replaced the coffee shop. For many people — those with disabilities, chronic illness, social anxiety, or who simply live far from the people they love — this shift was not just an inconvenience to be endured. It was, quietly, a revelation. They could now participate in ways that had previously been closed to them.

AI has embedded itself into this landscape in ways we barely notice. Platforms that use AI to moderate conversations in real time can reduce harassment and abuse, creating safer spaces for honest exchange. Intelligent transcription tools that make gatherings accessible to people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Scheduling assistants that take the friction out of coordination, making it easier for dispersed groups to stay close.

The mental health implications of all this are genuinely mixed, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. We know that screen-mediated connection carries its own costs. The slight flatness of video calls. The absence of the unconscious signals — a hand on an arm, a shared glance — that make in-person connection feel different. The way that being 'online' can feel like performing presence rather than inhabiting it.

The research consistently suggests that the quality of digital connection matters far more than the quantity. AI tools that reduce friction and expand access tend to support well-being. Algorithms that optimise purely for engagement — more time on the app, more clicks, more reactions — tend to undermine it. The difference is whether the tool is designed with the user's actual flourishing in mind, or with something else.

## Finding the Balance

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to end with a tidy conclusion that does not quite honour the complexity of all this.

The truth is: AI is not a substitute for human connection. It cannot replace the experience of being truly known by another person. It cannot replicate the warmth of sitting with someone in silence and feeling understood. These things matter, and no app — however well-designed, however empathetically trained — can fully replicate them.

But that is not the question. The question is whether AI can be a bridge — something that helps people move toward connection rather than away from it. And there, I think the answer is genuinely yes, with conditions attached.

Replika works best when it encourages users to also build and invest in human relationships. Woebot works best when it supplements professional care rather than replacing it. Hybrid gatherings work best when they complement, rather than permanently substitute for, being in the same room. The tools are not the problem. The intention behind them, and the awareness we bring to using them, is what matters.

We are, as the saying goes, co-evolving with this technology. It is learning to understand us. And perhaps, in the process, we are learning something about ourselves — about what we need, what we have been missing, and what kind of connection we are actually searching for.

That search, I am fairly sure, is one of the most human things there is.

So here is the question I want to leave you with — not as a challenge, but as a genuine invitation: *What kind of connection will you seek next?* Not the performed kind, not the scrolled kind, but the kind that actually fills something. AI might help you get there. Or it might simply remind you that, in the end, the destination was always other people. Either way, the search is worth it.
